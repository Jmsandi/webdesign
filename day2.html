Session 1What is digital literacy?

What is digital literacy?

Using a device doesn't automatically make someone fully digitally literate.

Digital literacy is

the ability to use digital devices, technology, applications and information correctly, safely and effectively.

A digitally literate person can gradually…

Operate devicesType and create informationCommunicate digitallyFind informationUse applicationsManage filesProtect personal informationSolve simple digital problems

Tutor notes

Make the point that using a device does not automatically mean someone is fully digitally literate.

Do not expect learners to recite the definition. Aim for a simple restatement in their own words.

How to explain it to students

Digital literacy means being able to use digital technology well, safely and sensibly. Many people can use a phone, but being digitally literate goes further.

It means you can operate devices, type, communicate, find information, use apps, keep your files organised, protect your personal information, and fix small problems. You will build these skills one at a time, starting this week.

Session 1Hardware and software

Hardware and software

Hardware

The physical parts you can touch.

KeyboardMouseMonitorLaptopHard driveFlash drive

Software

Programs and applications that run on a computer.

Google ChromeMicrosoft WordCalculatorWhatsAppPaintMedia players

Hardware is the physical machine. Software gives the machine instructions about what to do.

Tutor notes

Introduce this very simply. Learners at this level only need the basic idea.

Point at things and ask learners to classify them. Include a few that are slightly tricky, such as WhatsApp (software) and a flash drive (hardware).

How to explain it to students

Here's the simplest way to think about it. Hardware is anything you can touch: keyboard, mouse, monitor, flash drive. Software is the programs inside that tell the machine what to do: Chrome, Word, Calculator, WhatsApp.

Think of cooking. The pot and the stove are hardware. The recipe is software, because it tells you what to do with them.

Check: Is WhatsApp hardware or software? What about a flash drive?

Session 2Smartphones vs computers

Smartphones vs computers

Small and portableUsually larger

Tutor notes

Both are digital devices designed differently. Ask learners what they prefer each one for, and avoid saying that one is simply better.

Use the real devices side by side if you can.

How to explain it to students

A smartphone is small and easy to carry. You use it with your finger on a touchscreen, and it's great for calls and messaging. A computer is usually bigger. You normally use a keyboard and mouse, and it often has more storage and a larger screen. It's the usual choice for productivity and professional work.

They also run different apps: mobile apps on a phone, desktop apps on a computer.

Check: If you had to type a long report, which would you choose, and why?

Session 2Operating a computer

Switching a computer on

1Check that the computer has power
2Find the power button
3Press it once
4Wait for the system to load. Log in if asked
5Let the desktop appear before you open anything

Don't press the power button again and again while it starts.

Starting up takes a little time.

Tutor notes

Demonstrate each step slowly on a real computer, then have learners repeat it. Beginners often press the button repeatedly because nothing seems to happen, so reassure them that starting up takes time.

How to explain it to students

Let me show you. First check the computer has power: plugged in or charged. Find the power button and press it once. Just once.

Then be patient. The computer takes a little time to load. If it asks you to log in, do that. Wait until the desktop appears before you open anything.

Many beginners press the button again and again because nothing seems to happen. Please don't. Give it time.

Check: How many times do we press the power button?

Session 2Operating a computer

Switching a computer off correctly

1Open the Start menu or system menu
2Choose the power option
3Select Shut Down
4Wait until the device has turned off completely

Don't just remove the power.

An improper shutdown can sometimes cause unsaved work to be lost.

Tutor notes

Demonstrate the full sequence, then have each learner shut down their own machine. Do this at the end of every session so it becomes habit.

The reason to give: a computer should normally be shut down through its operating system, not by removing the power source. Improper shutdown can sometimes cause unsaved work to be lost.

How to explain it to students

Switching off has a right way too. Don't just pull out the plug or hold the power button. Open the menu, choose the power option, select Shut Down, and wait until the screen is completely off.

If you switch off the wrong way, you can lose work you haven't saved. It's like closing a shop properly instead of walking away and leaving the door open.

Session 2The mouse

Controlling the mouse

1Left button2Right button3Wheel
ClickPress the left button once.
Double-clickPress the left button twice, quickly.
Right-clickPress the right button once to show more options.
DragHold the button down while moving the mouse.
ScrollMove a page up or down with the wheel or touchpad.

Tutor notes

Introduce the pointer (cursor) first: the small arrow that follows the mouse. Demonstrate each action, then let learners copy you.

The syllabus specifically requires learners to practise drag, click and double-click. Double-click is usually the hardest, so be patient and demonstrate the rhythm.

How to explain it to students

On the screen you'll see a small arrow. That's the pointer. When you move the mouse, the pointer moves.

Click: press the left button once, to select something. Double-click: press the left button twice, quickly, to open something. Right-click: press the right button once to see extra options. Drag: hold the left button down, move the mouse, then let go. Scroll: roll the wheel to move a page up and down.

Double-click is the hardest one, so we will practise it a lot.

Session 2Applications

Opening and closing applications

An application, or app, is a program designed to do one particular task.

CalculatorGoogle ChromeMicrosoft WordPaintNotesMedia apps

Minimize

Temporarily hides the window.

Maximize

Makes the window fill most or all of the screen.

Close

Exits the application window.

Tutor notes

Demonstrate opening an app from the desktop or the application menu. Then show minimize, maximize, restore and close.

Practise with a simple app such as Calculator or Paint. Make sure learners can tell the difference between hiding a window and closing it. Opening and closing applications is one of the specified Week 1 practical activities.

How to explain it to students

An app is a program that does one job: Calculator for sums, Chrome for the internet, Word for documents, Paint for drawing. To open one, double-click its icon or find it in the menu.

Every app opens in a window with three buttons in a corner. Minimize hides the window for now. Maximize makes it fill the screen. Close shuts the app. After you maximize, you can restore the window to its smaller size.

Check: What is the difference between minimize and close?

Session 2Typing

Typing: good habits

Sit comfortably
Keep your back reasonably straight
Place the keyboard where it feels comfortable
Use both hands where possible
Press keys gently, not aggressively
Look at the screen regularly

Accuracy before speed.

You don't need to touch type yet. First get comfortable finding keys and entering text correctly.

Tutor notes

For absolute beginners, avoid demanding formal touch typing straight away. First build confidence in identifying keys and entering text correctly.

Watch posture and how hard learners press the keys, and gently correct as you walk around.

How to explain it to students

Before we type, a few good habits. Sit comfortably with your back reasonably straight. Put the keyboard where your arms feel relaxed. Use both hands if you can. Press the keys gently, you don't need to hit them. And look at the screen from time to time.

Most important: accuracy before speed. We are not racing. If you type slowly and correctly, speed will come with practice.
